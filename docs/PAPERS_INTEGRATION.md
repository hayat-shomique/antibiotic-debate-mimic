## 1. The four papers in one paragraph each

**Kim et al., *Capable language models can outgrow the benefits of collaboration*, Nature Machine Intelligence 8:1157–1172 (2026).** A controlled factorial evaluation of multi-agent coordination: 260 configurations built from six agentic benchmarks (web browsing, finance, Minecraft planning, workplace tool use, SWE-bench Verified, Terminal-Bench) × five coordination topologies × nine models from three vendors, holding task prompts, tool interfaces and per-system compute ceilings fixed and varying only coordination structure and model capability. Aggregated over everything, the mean multi-agent improvement over a single agent is 0.0% (95% CI −58.7% to 77.2%), with per-cell outcomes from +80.8% to −70.0%. One regression coefficient survives both cluster-robust inference and Holm–Bonferroni correction — the single-agent baseline (β̂ = 0.250, P_robust = 0.004, P_Holm = 0.018) — and a separately fitted baseline-by-team-size rule places a ~45% "capability-saturation" point above which added agents predict zero-to-negative gains. Weight it carries: high for the aggregate null and for the design template; moderate for the mechanism story (the coordination metrics are architecture-level constants applied uniformly across benchmarks, so they act as rescaled architecture labels in the regression); low for anything transported to a new domain, since their own leave-one-dataset-out cross-validation returns R² = −2.09 and they state the patterns "should not be interpreted as model-agnostic mechanisms beyond" the evaluated model range (p. 1170). The threshold is offered by the authors as a selection rule, not a scaling law, and the interaction term underneath it does not survive cluster-robust correction.

**Oh, Kim, Park & Kim, *Model and Task-Aware Test-Time Scaling Strategies for LLMs and VLMs in Medicine*, JMIR 28:e90693 (2026).** An evaluation study of test-time compute in medicine: ~30 open-weight models across five text benchmarks (5,578 items) and two multimodal benchmarks (7,000 items), under five configurations — no scaling, increased token budget (128 to 8192), iterative sequential self-revision, parallel sampling with a shortest-majority-vote rule, and a hybrid. Findings: non-reasoning models saturate below their budget and gain almost nothing; iterative sequential revision lowers accuracy on easy medical QA while parallel sampling raises it; the optimal strategy differs between two 7B medical reasoning models tested head to head; and when a misleading answer is injected as another physician's opinion, models abandon correct reasoning, with scaling recovering baseline on easier tasks such as MedQA but failing to fully restore it on difficult ones such as MedXpertQA. Weight it carries: high for the framing verdict (peer-reviewed, medical, explicit that scaling effects are conditional) and for the authority-perturbation precedent; low as a numeric source, because every primary result lives in a figure with no results table, there is no hypothesis test or multiplicity control anywhere in the main text, and the two headline experiments (sequential-vs-parallel, and the entire robustness analysis) rest on two 7B medical reasoning models.

**Zahavy, *Position: LLMs can't jump*, PMLR 306 (2026), venue line as printed in the PDF.** A position paper arguing that scientific invention needs Peirce's three inference modes, that generative AI has induction and is acquiring deduction, and that it has no mechanism for abduction — the generation of explanatory premises with no symbolic precedent. The case is one historical reconstruction (Einstein's route to General Relativity, 1907–1915, from secondary sources), an argument against creativity-as-compression, and a proposal that action-controllable world models are the missing substrate. Weight it carries: almost none as evidence. There is no study — no data, no models run, no metrics, and both figures are AI-generated illustrations. The author states the work is "not as a definitive proof, but as establishing a theoretical framework" (p. 8). Zero occurrences across the text of sycophancy, deference, persuasion, debate, multi-agent, confidence, calibration, clinical, medical, health, patient. It is background reading and a source of two framing lines, never related work, and never support for a measured finding. Disclosure a citing author should make: the author is at Google DeepMind, co-authors the AlphaProof paper he uses as evidence that deduction is being conquered, and the proposed remedy is his own institution's product line.

**Stürenburg et al., *Tracking funding disparities in global health aid with machine learning*, medRxiv preprint (2025), not peer reviewed.** An open-weight LLM (Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct-Turbo) is used as a one-shot document classifier over 3,713,935 OECD aid-project descriptions, assigning each to 17 Global Burden of Disease categories, after which per-capita aid rank is compared with disease-burden rank within income group. Communicable, maternal, neonatal and nutritional diseases took 97.5% of disease-specific aid; non-communicable diseases, carrying 59.5% of global DALYs, took 2.5%. The classifier reports accuracy 0.93 against 2,060 manual annotations, against a keyword baseline at 0.71 (macro recall 0.96 vs 0.44). Weight it carries on the research question: none — there is no agent, no interaction, no antibiotic-selection task, no organism-level reasoning, no susceptibility arbiter, no patient-level data. It carries weight for exactly one methodological move: demoting the outcome metric at the point of definition rather than in a limitations paragraph. Secondary uses are contrastive — the headline accuracy sits on a validation set that is 47.9% "Other" deployed on a corpus that is 91.3% non-disease, and the pipeline runs at temperature 0.7 / top-p 0.7 / top-k 50 with no seed reported and no repeated runs described, while naming "consistent classification" as a model-selection criterion (p. 25).

## 2. The frame

Kim et al. establish, on a controlled grid of 260 configurations, that coordination between language-model agents does not reliably outperform a single agent, and that the strongest available predictor of whether it helps is how well the single agent already does. Their aggregate is a mean improvement of 0.0%. Their one doubly-robust coefficient is the single-agent baseline. Everything else in their regression is demoted by their own statistical posture to descriptive directional patterns.

That is what makes a null-shaped result in this project a contribution rather than a failure. A finding that two agents finish with the accuracy of one is the direction a large, independently run, controlled prior already reports on general agentic tasks. Reporting it in a clinical frame is not a report of a broken experiment; it is a second, independent observation of the same direction in a domain their six benchmarks do not cover, obtained with a different metric family and a different arbiter.

[FACT] What their study establishes, precisely: a mean coordination effect indistinguishable from zero across their grid; a single-agent baseline coefficient that survives cluster-robust and Holm correction; a practical selection rule, validated by sign-match in 94% of 16 cells on two benchmarks, which the authors themselves scope as "a practical selection rule rather than a universal scaling principle."

[FACT] What this project's data can claim in relation to it: consistency of direction, obtained independently; a controlled-ablation design of the same shape (they hold prompts, tools and compute fixed and vary coordination structure and capability; this project holds model, quantization, temperature, seed, patient and prompt fixed and varies speaking order and challenge type, with an instruction-clause ablation that returned 60/60 adoption with and without the deference-inviting clause); a speaking-order axis, since none of the four verified items reports a speaking-order or agent-position effect; and an interaction-level failure mode — a fabricated clinical premise accepted by both participants, asserted on 13.8% of turns with zero organism mentions — for which their four-category taxonomy (logical contradiction, numerical drift, context omission, coordination failure, p. 1165) has no category. That extends their taxonomy; it does not refute it.

[FACT] What this project's data cannot claim, stated as the explicit list of ways this is **not** a test of their threshold:

1. **No baseline variation.** Locating or testing a threshold requires baselines spread across a grid. This project has one task, one metric, one model, one baseline.
2. **Out of the fitted range.** Their rule was fitted over success rates from 5% to 80% and tool counts from 2 to 16. A constant "always meropenem" policy scores 98.0% adequacy here, above their entire observed range, and the authors caution that the LODO cross-validation "cautions against extrapolating absolute baselines outside the tested domains" (p. 1170). Their rule therefore offers no calibrated prediction for this frame. The honest statement is that the observed null is not in tension with it.
3. **A different construct is being called "baseline".** Their baseline reflects task difficulty against a benchmark harness. The 98.0% here reflects a coarse adequacy metric over a closed 17-drug answer space in which broad-spectrum options cover nearly everything. The saturation is in the measurement, not in Qwen3-4B's clinical competence. That distinction is this project's contribution, not their finding.
4. **Sign rule, not effect size.** Their threshold predicts the sign of the coordination effect. It does not predict a magnitude, so no magnitude here can agree or disagree with it.
5. **Outside their model range.** Their nine models span intelligence index 42–71 from three frontier vendors. A 4-bit 4B model is outside that, and their scope statement — that the patterns "should not be interpreted as model-agnostic mechanisms beyond" the evaluated range — applies in reverse: this result cannot be attributed to their mechanism.
6. **One topology, not five.** They compare single-agent, independent, decentralized, centralized and hybrid. This project runs one two-agent peer exchange with no orchestrator and no vote rule. Their 87% architecture-selection accuracy and their ranking of topologies are untouched here. Their decentralized architecture resolves disagreement by plurality vote with a deterministic tie-break; this project's design has no vote and no tie-break rule, and the final drug tracks speaking order instead — that is a design difference to state, not a defect in their vote mechanism.
7. **No contribution to their validation set.** Their threshold validation is 16 model × benchmark cells on 20-instance subsets with bootstrap CIs of roughly ±20 pp, which they concede are underpowered pairwise. This project adds no cell to that set.
8. **Their coefficients may not be imported.** Their leave-one-dataset-out R² is −2.09, worse than predicting the mean. Nothing fitted in their Table 2 may be carried into a clinical setting.
9. **Non-overlapping outcome families.** Their primary outcome is task success, with four secondary metric families (validator-scored factual error rate, information gain, token-overlap and contradiction analysis, and efficiency/cost). Susceptibility concordance, spectrum appropriateness, escalation and de-escalation correctness, HRR, BCR and confidence before and after have no counterpart in that set. The correct-and-confident to wrong-and-confident transition the supervisor specified is not measurable in their framework.
10. **Not a clinical evaluation on their side.** Their six benchmarks are web browsing, finance, planning, workplace tool use, software engineering and CLI tasks (Table 3, p. 1166); none is clinical, and medical settings appear in the paper only as future work, named there as an embodied or multimodal case (p. 1167). This is an observation about their scope, and must not be presented as a statement by the authors that clinical settings were untested — the word "clinical" does not occur in their paper.

[OPINION] One asymmetry runs in this project's favour and should be said out loud. Their conservatism problem comes from having six dataset clusters, which inflates cluster-robust standard errors by up to 2.9× and demotes three of their effects to directional patterns. The clustering unit here is the patient, with G = 200, so cluster-robust inference is well behaved. The McNemar test (p = 3.0e-8) is a within-pair test and is unaffected; any run-level statistic should be clustered on patient and reported that way.

Language discipline throughout: alignment with observed microbiological outcomes, counterfactual appropriateness. MIMIC-IV is observational and no causal claim follows from any of this.

## 3. Does "reasoning enhancement" survive?

[FACT] Oh et al. conclude: "Test-time scaling rules from general domains do not perfectly translate to medical AI. Longer reasoning is not universally beneficial" (p. 1), and in the Discussion, "the efficacy of test-time scaling is highly conditional, varying significantly across model architectures, task difficulties, and clinical contexts" (p. 13).

[OPINION] Verdict: the original title survives only if it is read as a question about conditions. Any title that presupposes reasoning enhancement improves clinical decisions is not defensible on this evidence, and the presupposition is the part a supervisor or referee will attack first. A peer-reviewed 2026 JMIR paper states that more inference compute does not reliably buy medical performance, and this project's Phase 2 added inference compute and returned single-agent accuracy plus two failure modes — complete abandonment of the opening position under challenge, and order dependence of the final drug. So the umbrella is live; the promise inside it is not.

[FACT] Two further constraints from the same paper that shape the honest title. First, iterative sequential revision is a scaling strategy that lowered accuracy on the easiest task they tested: "On PubMedQA, the easiest QA task, both models show a decline in accuracy as the number of sequential sampling steps increases" (p. 11). A debate is sequential revision with a second speaker, so a title claiming enhancement would be claiming the opposite of their easy-task result. Second, their authority-perturbation result is difficulty-scoped: scaling recovers baseline on easier tasks such as MedQA (p. 13) and "even optimal scaling strategies fail to restore baseline performance" under expert-framed misleading hints on the hard end (p. 14). Adding inference compute is not the general fix, but it is also not uniformly useless, and a title must not claim either extreme.

**Title the evidence supports:** *When does added inference help? Two-agent antibiotic debate scored against laboratory susceptibility.*

Fallback, if a declarative is required: *Order-dependent capitulation in a two-agent antibiotic debate, scored against S/I/R.*

[DISSENT] Do not title the talk with the capitulation rate alone. The 100% abandonment figure is arresting but it is one configuration, one 4B non-reasoning model, one task, and Oh et al. show two 7B medical reasoning models with opposite optimal strategies (p. 14). The title should carry the question and the arbiter; the rate belongs on slide 6.

[FACT] Named exposure, since it will be asked: Oh et al. identify parallel sampling with a shortest-majority-vote rule (score = category count divided by the log of that category's mean solution length) as the superior strategy on easier tasks (pp. 13–14). This project has not run a self-consistency arm. Running k independent single-agent samples at temperature 0.7, aggregated by that length-penalised rule and scored on the same S/I/R arbiter, converts a likely referee question into a result. Plain majority vote is a different method from theirs and should not be described as reproducing their arm.

## 5. Claims this project may now make, and claims it may not

### May make

1. **The observed null coordination effect is consistent with a large controlled prior.** Kim et al., mean multi-agent improvement 0.0% (95% CI −58.7% to 77.2%) across 260 configurations (p. 1159). Phrase as "consistent with", never "confirms".
2. **Where the single agent already stands is the best-supported predictor of whether coordination helps.** Kim et al., single-agent baseline β̂ = 0.250, P_robust = 0.004, P_Holm = 0.018 (Table 2, p. 1163) — the only coefficient surviving both corrections.
3. **The 400 ordering-runs constitute a controlled ablation.** Kim et al., p. 1158, identical prompts, tools and compute budgets with a single varied factor, cited as design precedent.
4. **Compute matching is the fairness control a reader will look for.** Kim et al., p. 1170. If the single-agent baseline was not given the two-agent token budget, say so; their own caveat that "we did not optimize prompts specifically for each model or model family" (p. 1167) is the accompanying limit on how far a matched-compute claim reaches.
5. **Speaking order is an axis none of the four verified items analyses.** Kim et al. report no speaking-order or agent-position effect anywhere; their decentralized topology resolves disagreement by plurality vote with a deterministic tie-break (p. 1169), so the comparison to state is that this project's design has no vote and no tie-break rule.
6. **Cluster-robust inference is well behaved here.** Kim et al. concede cluster-robust SEs "are known to be conservative" with G = 6 (p. 1168); the clustering unit here is the patient, G = 200.
7. **Added inference compute is not a general repair for authority-induced abandonment, and the effect is difficulty-scoped.** Oh et al.: recovery to baseline on easier tasks such as MedQA (p. 13), and on the hard end "even optimal scaling strategies fail to restore baseline performance" (p. 14). Both halves must be stated together.
8. **Longer reasoning is not universally beneficial in medicine.** Oh et al., p. 1, Conclusions.
9. **Sequential self-revision can move a model away from a correct starting answer on easy medical tasks.** Oh et al., p. 11 (PubMedQA decline, read from Figure 6) and pp. 13–14: "Iterative sequential scaling proved harmful on easier datasets such as PubMedQA. This performance drop is likely due to compounding errors during unnecessarily extended reasoning. In this case, parallel scaling using majority voting yielded superior results."
10. **A coverage-minus-accuracy statistic separates "never had the adequate drug" from "had it and revised away from it".** Adapted from Oh et al.'s coverage definition (p. 8) — their coverage is the proportion of correct answers appearing anywhere in intermediate outputs; the version proposed here is a proportion over ordering-runs. Say "adapted from", not "imported". Their supporting observation is m1-specific and inferred from a coverage/accuracy divergence rather than trace inspection, so write "consistent with", not "the mechanism is".
11. **The claimed clinical expertise of the source changes model behaviour, which licenses persona as a substantive endpoint.** Oh et al., p. 13: "Notably, models are particularly sensitive to the expertise level of the physician providing the additional input." Persona only — this licenses nothing about speaking order.
12. **A metric should be demoted where it is defined.** Stürenburg et al., p. 27, as a precedent for stating in the Methods that susceptibility concordance measures alignment with a recorded microbiological panel and not clinical correctness.
13. **A cheap non-model baseline belongs beside the model.** Stürenburg et al., Table S5 (p. 60): macro recall 0.96 for the LLM framework against 0.44 for the keyword baseline, with musculoskeletal precision and recall both 0.00 for the baseline. That is what a capability gain over a degenerate comparator looks like; the always-meropenem 98.0% comparator here is not beaten.
14. **This project's decoding discipline is stricter than at least one recent LLM-annotation pipeline.** Stürenburg et al. run temperature 0.7 / top-p 0.7 / top-k 50 (p. 66), report no seed, describe no repeated runs and no run-to-run agreement statistic, while naming "consistent classification" as one of three model-selection criteria (p. 25). Phrase as "this pipeline does not fix the decoding", never as a claim about the field.
15. **The task sits inside a closed, well-precedented answer space, analogous to the regime Zahavy concedes to current models, and even there the opening recommendation shows almost no alignment with patient-specific input.** Zahavy, p. 2 (deduction as Rule + Case → Result) and p. 1 / p. 5 (the concession). Label the move an analogy: his concession is grounded in proof-checked formal domains (Lean, mathlib, AlphaProof), plus a hedged "plausible" claim about spotting contradictions in existing scientific literature.
16. **Fluent domain language emitted with no referent in the supplied case extends an existing stated position on ungrounded symbols.** Zahavy, p. 7. Extension, not instantiation — his grounding is sensory and bodily, a chart value is symbolic data.

### May not make, and what would earn each

1. *"Our result confirms (or refutes) the ~45% capability-saturation threshold."* Would need baselines varying across a grid of tasks inside their fitted range (success rates 5–80%), several models, and per-cell sign predictions. One task, one baseline, one model cannot locate a threshold.
2. *"The debate caused worse antibiotic decisions."* MIMIC-IV is observational. Would need an interventional design. Permitted phrasing: alignment with observed microbiological outcomes; counterfactual appropriateness.
3. *"Peer debate does not provide verification."* Kim et al.'s 22.7% error-reduction sentence (p. 1165) has an ambiguous referent — "These architectures" most likely covers centralized, hybrid and decentralized, given that the following sentence contrasts independent MAS at +4.6% amplification — so a two-agent no-orchestrator setup is not a clean counterexample to a decentralized-specific claim. "Verification-bearing architectures" is not their phrase and does not occur in their paper. Permitted form: their figure is measured on six agentic benchmarks with frontier models and does not reproduce in this clinical frame. Earning more would need a verification-manipulation arm run across their architectures.
4. *"Terminal agreement between our agents is evidence against answer quality, per their r = −0.136."* Construct mismatch: their R is work redundancy, the mean cosine similarity of agent output embeddings during coordination, not terminal agreement on a final answer. It explains under 2% of variance, and their framing is a trade-off with an optimum at R ≈ 0.41, with a positive redundancy × agent-count interaction (β̂ = 0.024, naive P = 0.034, Table 2). Keep as a loose analogy at most. Earning the claim would need our own measured redundancy–outcome relation.
5. *"Kim et al. state that clinical settings were not evaluated."* No such statement exists; "clinical" does not occur in their paper, and medical triage appears only in a future-work sentence classed as embodied or multimodal. Permitted replacement: their six benchmarks (Table 3, p. 1166) are web browsing, finance, planning, workplace tasks, software engineering and CLI tasks; none is clinical, and medical triage is named only as future work (p. 1167).
6. *"Test-time scaling cannot repair authority-induced capitulation."* Contradicted by Oh et al., p. 13: on relatively easy tasks both models recover performance comparable to the unperturbed condition. Permitted form is the difficulty-scoped one in claim 7 above.
7. *"Oh et al. license our order effect."* Their main text contains no occurrence of order effect, ordering, agent, multi-agent or debate. Earning it would need a study that manipulates speaker order, or this project's own result stated as the first such observation among the verified items.
8. *"All ground truth in Oh et al. is a multiple-choice key."* False for MedCalc-Bench, whose answers are decimal, integer, date or time (Table 1, p. 4), scored within a 5% error margin (p. 7). Permitted form: six of the seven benchmarks are multiple choice; MedCalc-Bench is numeric with a tolerance. The S/I/R-arbiter contribution survives either way.
9. *"Their token-saturation result explains why our opening recommendation barely varies with the patient."* They measured token count and accuracy against budget, not response-content invariance across inputs. No inference about case-conditioning follows. If the connection is wanted at all it is an [ASSUMPTION] with no citation attached.
10. *"Our confabulation rate instantiates Zahavy's symbol-grounding claim."* His grounding is sensory and bodily; a white-cell count is symbolic data that was present or absent. It is an extension. Earning "instantiates" would need a grounding manipulation.
11. *"Zahavy limits his thesis to physics, so it does not reach clinical settings."* He limits the world-model proposal (p. 8) and holds the abductive-jump claim universal (p. 7, on manipulative abduction extending beyond physics). Permitted form: his positive programme is tailored to the physical sciences and does not transfer to a clinical setting, which is why the paper is background rather than related work.
12. *Any numeric accuracy figure attributed to Oh et al.* Their main text prints no results table; Tables 3 and 4 are prose recommendations. Every primary result must be read off a figure, so nothing quantitative from that paper can serve as a numeric baseline. Earning it would need their Multimedia Appendix 1 or the underlying data.
13. *"Stürenburg et al. (published)."* It is a medRxiv preprint carrying the standard non-certification footer. Also, their accuracy 0.93 appears twice within one Methods sentence (p. 26) and in Table S5 (p. 60); the Results section reports the validation with no number. Do not claim the number is repeated in the Results.
14. *"Their coefficients predict our setting."* Their LODO R² = −2.09, worse than predicting the mean on an unseen domain (p. 1168).
15. *Zahavy's sense of "counterfactual" anywhere near ours.* His is Pearl-style intervention on a simulator inside a "synthetic laboratory" (p. 7). Ours is scoring a recommendation against a susceptibility panel recorded under different actual treatment. Same word, different object; letting his phrasing in would break the project's own writing rule.
16. *"At n = 2 their vote mechanism cannot resolve a split."* Their rule includes a deterministic tie-break, which does resolve a split. Permitted form: our design has no vote and no tie-break rule, and the final answer tracks speaking order.
17. *A direct citation of Harnad (1990) or of Lim et al. (2026), reference [45] in Oh et al.* Neither PDF has been read. Reading them is what would earn the citation.
